Study Abroad Counselling

University shortlisting, SOP and LOR review, application filing, scholarship search and visa documentation for 15+ countries.

Guides from this practice

Most rejections we see are not ability problems. They are list problems: eight universities picked from a ranking table, all in the same competitiveness band, none matched to the applicant's actual profile.

A list that converts is built in three tiers:

  • Ambitious (2–3): your profile sits below the median admit, but you have one genuine differentiator — a publication, a patent, a strong recommender, unusual work experience.
  • Target (3–4): your GPA, test scores and experience sit at or just above the published median admit profile.
  • Safe (2): you clear the stated minimum comfortably, and you would genuinely accept the offer. If you would not attend, it is not a safety, it is a wasted fee.

Then check each entry against four things the rankings will not tell you: is there a faculty member working on your topic, does the department fund international students, what is the visa approval trend for that consulate, and can you afford year two without a scholarship.

Rule of thumb: if you cannot name the specific lab, professor or course module that made you add a university, remove it.

Get your shortlist reviewed →

An admissions reader spends roughly two minutes on a first pass. The structure below survives that pass.

  • 1. The hook, in specifics. One concrete moment from your work or research, not "since childhood I have been fascinated by".
  • 2. What you have actually done. Projects, results, numbers. A claim without evidence reads as filler.
  • 3. The gap. Name the thing you cannot learn where you are. This is the only paragraph that justifies the application.
  • 4. Why this department. Two named faculty, one named course or facility, and a sentence on how it connects to paragraph three.
  • 5. What happens after. A plausible five-year plan. Ambitious is fine; vague is not.

Things to cut on the second draft: adjectives about yourself, quotes from famous people, the word "esteemed", and any sentence that would be true of a hundred other applicants.

Get an SOP review →

Work backwards from the intake, not forwards from today.

  • 12 months out: book IELTS/TOEFL and GRE/GMAT. Start the shortlist.
  • 9 months: tests done. Approach recommenders now, while they still remember your work.
  • 7 months: SOP draft one. Financial documents opened, because most consulates want a seasoned balance.
  • 5–6 months: applications submitted. Early rounds have better funding odds than later ones.
  • 3–4 months: offers arrive; compare total cost, not tuition. Accept and pay the deposit.
  • 2 months: I-20 or CAS, SEVIS or IHS fee, visa slot. Slots are the bottleneck — book the day you are eligible.
  • 1 month: visa interview, accommodation, forex, flights.

The two steps that most often blow the deadline are recommender delays and visa slot availability. Both are outside your control, so start them early.

Academic Writing & Thesis

Research structuring, chapter editing, data analysis support, journal formatting and plagiarism checks. Guidance, not ghost-writing.

Guides from this practice

A desk rejection lands in days, before a single reviewer reads the science. Nearly all of them come from four causes.

  • Scope mismatch. The paper is fine; the journal never publishes this topic. Check the last two years of issues, not the aims-and-scope page.
  • No stated contribution. If the abstract does not say what is new in one sentence, an editor will not go looking for it.
  • Formatting. Reference style, figure resolution, word count, ethics statement, data availability. Unglamorous and decisive.
  • Weak cover letter. Three short paragraphs: what the paper shows, why this journal's readers need it, and confirmation it is unpublished and unsubmitted elsewhere.

Practical order: finalise results → write the contribution sentence → pick the journal → write to that journal's template → then write the cover letter. Picking the journal last is how most first drafts get rejected twice.

Get a pre-submission check →

Supervisors return chapters for structural reasons far more often than for wrong results. Give every chapter the same skeleton and the reviews get shorter.

  • Opening (½ page): what this chapter answers and how it follows from the last one.
  • Method: enough detail for someone else to reproduce it. Parameters in a table, not in prose.
  • Results: figures first, then the text that reads them. Each figure earns its place or it moves to an appendix.
  • Discussion: compare against published work by number, not by adjective. "18% higher than Sharma et al. (2022)" beats "significantly better".
  • Closing (½ page): the finding in one line, plus the question the next chapter picks up.

Write the opening and closing of every chapter last. Read all of them in sequence — that is your thesis argument, and if it does not hold together there, it will not hold in the viva.

Get a chapter reviewed →

A similarity report is a map, not a verdict. A 22% score made up of references, standard method phrasing and institutional boilerplate is usually fine. A 6% score containing one uncited paragraph from someone else's discussion is not.

Read the report source by source:

  • Exclude the bibliography and quoted material, then re-run.
  • Any single source above roughly 3–5% deserves a look.
  • Matches inside your own earlier papers are self-plagiarism and still need citation.
  • Method sections legitimately repeat standard language; discussion sections should not.

UGC-affiliated Indian institutions generally apply a 10% ceiling, but the exact rule is your university's, not the software's. Ask your department for their written policy before you optimise for a number.

Career Development

Résumé rewrite, LinkedIn optimisation, mock interviews and a career roadmap built around where you want to be in three years.

Guides from this practice

Two readers, two very different tests. The parser needs plain structure; the human needs evidence.

For the parser: single column, standard section headings, no text inside images or headers, a .docx or a text-layer PDF, and the exact job-title vocabulary used in the posting.

For the human: every bullet is verb → what you did → measurable result.

  • Weak: "Responsible for improving the analysis process."
  • Strong: "Rebuilt the load-analysis workflow in Python, cutting model runtime from 6 hours to 40 minutes across 30 projects."

If a bullet has no number, ask what changed because you were there — time saved, cost avoided, error rate, headcount, revenue, or scale. One page per ten years of experience. Photos, marital status and date of birth come off for anything outside India.

Get a résumé rewrite →

Recruiter search weighs a handful of fields far more than the rest. Fix these and profile views usually move within a fortnight.

  • Headline: role plus specialism plus tools. "Structural Engineer · Seismic Design · ETABS, SAP2000" beats "Aspiring engineer | Open to work".
  • Current job title: use the industry-standard name, not your company's internal designation.
  • Skills: the top three are pinned and searchable. Order them for the job you want next.
  • About: first two lines show before the "see more" fold. Put the specialism and the proof there.

Then give the algorithm a reason to show you: one substantive post a fortnight about work you actually did outperforms fifty reshares.

Get a profile audit →

"Tell me about a time you handled conflict" is not a personality test. It is a request for evidence, and it has a shape.

  • Situation (1 sentence): just enough context.
  • Task (1 sentence): what was on you specifically.
  • Action (3–4 sentences): what you decided and did. This is the answer; everything else is scaffolding.
  • Result (1–2 sentences): the outcome, with a number if one exists, and what you would do differently.

Prepare six stories, not sixty answers: a conflict, a failure, a deadline you saved, something you led, something you learned fast, something you shipped. Almost every behavioural question maps onto one of them. Ninety seconds each — practise with a timer, because the most common failure mode is a good story told for four minutes.
